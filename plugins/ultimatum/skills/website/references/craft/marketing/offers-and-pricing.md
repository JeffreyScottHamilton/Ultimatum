# Offers and pricing

Read when the owner asks what to charge, how to package services, how to build a pricing page, how to make an offer more appealing, or what to give away for an email address. The owner sets every price and term; you lay out options and their trade-offs.

## 1. Shape the offer

An offer is everything the customer gets and risks, not just the price. Before touching the page, write it down:

| Part | Ask |
|---|---|
| Result | What changes for the customer, and by when? |
| Contents | What exactly is included? What isn't? |
| Effort | What must the customer do (prepare, attend, wait)? Less is worth more. |
| Speed | How soon do they see the first result? |
| Risk | What happens if it doesn't work out: refund, redo, cancel any time? |
| Price | One number, a "from" number, or a quote, and what it is per (job, month, hour, person) |
| Reason to act now | A real one only: limited slots this month, a seasonal deadline, a price rise on a date the owner commits to |

Most weak offers are fixed by making the result more specific, cutting the customer's effort or lowering their risk, not by discounting.

**Honest urgency and scarcity.** Only what is true: a real count of slots, a real end date that holds, a real price change. Countdown timers that reset, "only 2 left" that never changes, and fake "someone just bought" pop-ups break consumer protection law in many places and lose trust when spotted. The compliance reviewer flags them.

**Bonuses.** Add one only if it removes a real next problem (a care guide after a repair, a first follow-up session). Price it honestly or don't price it at all.

## 2. Pricing

The owner decides. Help them by setting out:

- **What it's per.** Pick the unit that grows with the value the customer gets: per room cleaned, per guest, per month of service, per project. A unit the customer can predict beats one that surprises them.
- **Where it sits.** List three competitors' prices with the date checked and the source page. Price follows what the customer compares you with, not what it costs you, but costs set the floor.
- **Packages.** Two or three, each for a different customer, not a cut-down and a padded version of one. Name them by who they're for ("Single room", "Whole home", "Move-out clean").
- **The middle choice.** When there are three, the middle is usually the one most should pick; mark it once ("Most booked") only if that's true.
- **Changes.** A rise for existing customers gets notice, a reason and a date; keep loyal customers on the old price for a while if the owner can afford it.

Small tests the owner can run without a statistics tool: quote the new price to the next ten enquiries and count the yeses; offer two packages and see which gets picked.

## 3. Guarantees

A guarantee lowers the customer's risk, so it only works if it's specific and the owner will honour it every time.

- Say exactly what is promised and what the customer must do to claim ("If the leak returns within 90 days, we fix it free. Call the number on your invoice.").
- Keep conditions few and printed next to the promise, not in a separate terms page.
- Local law may already give customers rights (refunds, cooling-off periods for distance sales). A guarantee can't take those away; check before relying.

## 4. The pricing page

- Prices visible. "Contact us for pricing" loses most visitors; if a quote is truly needed, give a "from" price or a typical range. `ult market page --kind pricing` warns when no price is shown.
- Each package: who it's for, the price and its unit, 3 to 6 included items in plain words, one button with the same label pattern ("Book a whole-home clean").
- A short comparison table only when packages differ in more than three ways.
- Answer the pricing worries right below: extras and call-out fees, how payment works, cancelling, what happens if the job runs over.
- One real review that mentions value or price.
- Taxes: say whether prices include them. Monthly and yearly: show both clearly; don't show a yearly price divided by twelve without saying it's billed yearly.

## 5. Lead offers (something free for an email)

Worth it when buyers take a while to decide (weddings, renovations, B2B services). Not needed for "I need a plumber today".

| Offer | Fits | Cost to make |
|---|---|---|
| Checklist or price guide | planning purchases ("What a kitchen fit costs in 2026") | low |
| Short email course | skills, coaching, software | medium |
| Calculator or quiz | quotes with a few variables, product fit | medium; a free tool can also earn links |
| Free consultation or sample | high-trust services | owner's time |
| Template or swipe file | B2B, creators | low |

Rules: solve one specific problem the buyer has before they buy; lead naturally to the paid service; deliver instantly; ask only for an email (name optional). Say what they'll receive and how often you'll email. Consent rules apply (`launch-and-email.md` §4).

**Free tools.** A calculator or checker that answers a common question ("How much paint do I need?") can bring search traffic and links for years. Build it only if it's simple, answers a real searched question, and sits next to what the owner sells. Keep the answer free; ask for an email only to send a saved copy or a detailed report.
