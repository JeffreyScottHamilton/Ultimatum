# Voice, registers and copy moves

Read at step two with `copy.md`, once you know the buyer. Here you settle the content of the words and their sound.

## Why model copy reads as model copy

- One voice for every client, usually warm, wry and literary.
- The same devices in every section.
- Sincerity stated ("we care") instead of shown.
- Invented detail, with the same names recurring.

Professional copy differs: true details, each device used once, the client's own voice, real limits, and uneven sections. So swapping in synonyms fixes nothing; adding facts and making choices does.

## Fact sheet

Fill this before writing. An empty field shrinks or removes its section; never fill it with "coming soon".

| Field | Holds | Goes to |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | category in plain words | `h1` |
| For whom | the buyer, as they'd describe themselves | sub-line, features |
| Today's alternative | what they use now | features, FAQ |
| True difference | the one thing others can't say | hero, close |
| Customer words | phrases from reviews, calls, emails | features, proof |
| Numbers | prices, counts, times, sizes with units | first screen, features |
| People | who runs it, named only if given | story, footer |
| Odd details | the specific thing only this business has | story, cards |
| A limit | what it doesn't do | FAQ, story |
| An enemy | the habit or problem it fights | sub-line, features |
| The offer | what happens when they click | buttons, close |

- A thin brief is not a reason to stop. When a person asked for copy, ask up to six questions in one message; otherwise write with what you have and list the gaps.
- Limits and enemies come from the brief or well-known trade facts. An invented flaw is still invented.

## Voice card

Pick one register (below) by buyer, price and character. When torn, choose whichever is furthest from your previous page. A writing sample or old site from the client overrides the menu. Write the card on the plan, never on the page:

```
Voice:     technical and dry
Pairs:     borrows one plain admission from "facts only"
Person:    we / you
Sentences: short; figures as numerals; one long sentence per section at most
Humour:    none
House words: "run", "branch", "preview" (the product's own nouns)
Never:     superlatives, "seamless", rhetorical questions
```

- Test it: picture the same voice on nine other trades. If it would suit even one, keep tuning it toward this client until it would not.
- Voice holds, tone moves: hero and story carry the voice; prices, FAQ, booking and forms stay plain.
- No register bends a rule in `copy.md`.

## Registers

The model's default voice (warm, wry, literary, against big companies) is not used unless the client requests it. Choose from these:

| # | Register | Moves | Fits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Facts only | plain statements, names, dates, counts | professional services, B2B, clubs | the facts are weak |
| 2 | Technical and dry | label plus figure, real commands, the enemy as a category | developer tools, APIs | it becomes a spec dump |
| 3 | Founder's letter | first person, a named problem, one refrain, signed in text | products with a stance | the stance is put on |
| 4 | Self-aware playful | straight face, the joke in the sub-line, no hype even as a joke | drinks, snacks, studios | jokes aim at customers or at money and health |
| 5 | Literary formal | raised vocabulary, "clients", exact sensory lists, plain price | skincare, design objects | ornate without being exact |
| 6 | Proper nouns and restraint | places, history, quiet verbs; never the word "luxury" | fine hotels, heritage, galleries | there is no real place or history |
| 7 | Sensory, second person, present | literal sensory facts, one absence, odd numbers | cafés, gyms, spas | every section turns sensory |
| 8 | Historical scene | a dated opening, named streets and people | restaurants, family firms | the history is generic |
| 9 | Plain-spoken activist | one measurable mission, admits harm, proof in numbers | mission brands, charities | the mission is an abstract noun |
| 10 | Refusal and narrowness | defined by what it won't do, honest scarcity | makers, specialists | the refusal is invented |
| 11 | Product as hero | the product is the subject; a spec follows every turn of phrase | hardware, one-feature apps | wordplay has no fact under it |
| 12 | Hurried conversational | talks to one busy person, short asides, self-deprecation | freelancers, small studios, founders | chummy, or jokes replace information |

## Order and length

Answer the reader's questions in order: what is it, is it for me, why believe it, how it works, what it costs, what if it fails, what now.

- How you open depends on what the reader knows: unaware (name the problem), knows the problem (name the fix), comparing (the difference), knows the client (the offer).
- A section exists only when there is true material for it. Five real sections beat eight padded ones. Counts follow content: four features, one quote, two plans.
- One organising idea may break the template once (say, a single object, a letter from the founder, a timetable, or the menu itself as the page). Otherwise vary the form: lists, tables, a letter, a paragraph.

## Copy moves

**Hero.** 2–9 words, one clause, no stacked adjectives. Forms that work:

| Form | Shape | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Category and buyer | "Payroll for restaurants" | most software and services |
| Place | "Bike repairs in Leith" | local businesses |
| Twist | "Accounting that closes itself" | the twist is literally true |
| Customer | "For physios who hate admin" | a sharp buyer |
| Scope | "Every lock in the house" | breadth is the point |
| Year | "Mending shoes since 1962" | a real long history |
| Superlative | "The lightest tent we've made" | a measurable claim |
| Name and genre | "Saltmarsh, a fishing game" | games |

Greetings, moods, questions, commands, enemies, idioms and news never take the `h1`; they fit the sub-line, a section opener, the close or the brand line.

**Sub-lines.** Describe the reader's state ("Your inbox, sorted before you open it"), name the enemy as a habit ("Booking forms that ask for your life story"), or state news plainly ("New frame, same price").

**Headings.** Default: the part's name, with a claim line under it. Or verb and thing ("Send invoices from the van"); or a plain claim ("Staff paid the same day"); or a figure from the brief ("Eleven routes, reset every Monday").

**Features.** Mechanism in verb-led steps; outcome first with the feature as its proof; a specific moment instead of a use case ("At 6 pm the till totals itself"). Real values: times, sizes, amounts, never "customisable". Interface fragments carry the client's data (`cards-and-ui.md`). Names of rooms, plans and models stay identical everywhere. Card length follows what you know.

**About.** Begin with a specific moment from the brief rather than a statement of belief. Anecdotes, timelines, real questions, never value pillars. Include failure and luck if the brief has them. Founders as people, named only if given. Values as behaviours with numbers. End in a modest present tense, then say how to reach them. A portfolio says what the person does, the work with dates, one belief and when they're free.

**Proof.** Only from the brief: verbatim quotes with name and role, kept rough as written; sourced figures; permitted logos. Let others say the superlatives. A rule can prove craft ("Every joint is brazed, never glued"). Craft, luxury and small places may have no proof section at all.

**Prices, FAQ, booking.** Plain facts. Price perks literally ("Late checkout until 2 pm, £15"). Put risk reversal beside the button only when it's true. Admit limits in the FAQ and answer the buyer's real objection flatly ("Yes, you can export everything as CSV").

**Buttons and close.** A verb and a real object, literal even when the register plays. Give people still browsing a soft way out ("See the rooms first"). The close repeats the hero's promise, adds one fact, and uses exactly the same main-action label.

**Microcopy.** Write it wherever it exists: a form error that says what to do ("We're closed Mondays, pick another day"), a sent message naming the request and the reply time, an empty state ("Your first invoice lands here"), a 404 pointing to the menu and hours, a newsletter confirmation with the real rhythm, a field hint only for what the brief promises.

**Numbers and humour.** Specific, unround, human-scale numbers from the brief work better than big round ones; each sits in a sentence next to its claim. Humour points at ourselves, lives in asides and microcopy, and stays out of serious purchases. A rhythm device (short scene sentences, a list ending on a surprise) once per page.

**Footer.** Facts only: legal row, street, district, hours. No sign-off.

Per page. Over budget is a warning; double the budget plus one is a failure.

## Over-correction

Removing one habit often breeds another. Watch for:

| New habit to watch for | Appears after the rule | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| comma splices | No em dashes | use full stops |
| lists of two or four everywhere | No lists of three | count the real items |
| flat noun piles | No adjectives | keep the one adjective that is a fact |
| every heading a command | No questions | use noun headings |
| telegraphic fragments | short sentences | let meaning set the length |
| hedging ("one of the") | No superlatives | state the measured fact |
| stiffness in microcopy | No jokes | plain and kind |
| sensory overload | show, don't tell | one sensory line per section |
| invented specifics | use specifics | only brief facts |
| random lengths | vary rhythm | vary with content |

## Checks

1. Lint the text (the anti-generic lint in `../../website/design-rules.md` §2 and `copy.md` § Tells). Fix failures; fix or justify warnings.
2. Rival test: rewrite any line that still fits a competitor, using the fact sheet.
3. Sameness: compare headings across your recent pages; rewrite repeats.
4. Read aloud as the client; cut what they wouldn't say.
5. Look for the over-correction that replaced the habit you removed.
6. Give the page text only to a fresh reader (a subagent) and ask what the business is, who it's for and what to do next. Do not rewrite to get past AI-detection tools.

The report carries the one-line voice card, every assumed value and what the client must still supply.
