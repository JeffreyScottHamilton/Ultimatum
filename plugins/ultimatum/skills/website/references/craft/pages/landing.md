# Landing pages: the questions the page must answer

Read when writing or reviewing a marketing page, a product page or the home page of a small business. For the shape of the page, `../hallmark/page-shapes.md`; for the hero itself, `../hallmark/hero-and-page.md`. This file is about content order and jobs.

## 1. A visitor arrives with questions

A visitor scrolls while silently asking a run of questions. A page works when each one is answered by the time it is asked. The usual order:

| # | The visitor wonders | The page answers with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is this, and is it for me? | a headline that names the category and the person it serves, with a plain sentence under it |
| 2 | Is it real? | the product itself, early: the interface, the output, the food, the room, the work |
| 3 | Does anyone use it? | named customers, reviews with names, counts with a source and a date |
| 4 | How would I start? | the first steps, numbered, from today to a first result |
| 5 | What else does it do? | a few claims, each next to a real piece of the product |
| 6 | Can I trust it? | the actual controls, guarantees, limits and policies, named |
| 7 | What does it cost? | the real price and what it is per, or a clear "quote on request" |
| 8 | Is it alive? | recent work, a changelog, news with dates, opening hours that are current |
| 9 | But what about...? | the real objections, answered plainly (a short FAQ) |
| 10 | Fine, what now? | the main action again, saying what it does |

Leave a question out when the business cannot answer it truthfully yet; an empty slot filled with vague text costs more trust than a missing section. A short announcement bar above everything is fine for something genuinely new and dated, then remove it.

## 2. Navigation

- Four or five items at the top, the last one being the main action. Price and contact (or docs, for software) are never more than one click away.
- The logo goes home. On phones the menu keeps the main action visible outside the drawer.
- A product with several big parts can give its own page a second, sticky row of links for that page only.

## 3. Actions come in pairs

- Pair one action that commits (book, buy, sign up, get a key) with one that informs (see the menu, watch the two-minute tour, read the docs). Someone not ready to commit still has somewhere to go.
- The committing button names what happens: "Book a table", "Start a 14-day trial". "Get started" alone hides it.
- One accent colour marks the main action everywhere on the page; other strong colours live inside photos, data and product content, not on buttons and chrome.

## 4. Show what people cannot picture

Pick the device by what the visitor is missing:

| They cannot picture | Show |
|---|---|
| it working for their case | tabs or a switch per use case, each with genuinely different content |
| what happens behind the scenes | a diagram whose arrows carry real conditions or values |
| the change it makes | before and after on the same task, or a short "a day with" story |
| how many people use it | numbers with units, scope, source and date, or a live list |
| what it feels like | the real thing to try, or a recording at a size that can be read |

If switching tabs only changes a sentence, or the diagram's boxes carry no labels that matter, the device teaches nothing; remove it.

## 5. Type and colour budget

- One text family, plus a monospace only for real code and values. If there is a third face it appears once, as the headline or one brand statement.
- Three colours do the work: the page colour, the text colour and the accent. Sections are told apart by slightly lighter or darker page colour.
- Big display type often needs tighter letter spacing; check line breaks at phone width.

## 6. Make it this business's page

If every answer uses the most common pattern, the page reads as a template. Answer at least one question in a way only this business could: their own photo instead of stock, their actual numbers, the founder's sentence, a tool visitors can use right on the page. `../hallmark/direction.md` covers choosing what makes it theirs.

## 7. First-screen test

Look at the first screen without scrolling, on desktop and on a phone, then answer:

1. Could a stranger say what this is and who it is for within five seconds?
2. Can you point to something on screen that the business actually made, sells or did?
3. Does the main button say what it does, and is it the only accent?
4. With motion turned off and before any scroll effect runs, is the content all there?
5. What on this screen could not be on a competitor's page?

Any "no" is a fix before hand-over. The page linter (`../design-review-qa/page-lint.md`) catches the mechanical slips; these five need a person or a screenshot.
