# Brand voice and messaging

Read at step 2 of `brand-flow.md`, before any visual work, when a brand needs its way of speaking and its core messages written down. For the craft of page copy itself (registers, copy moves, length) use `../taste/voice.md` and `../taste/copy.md`; this file sets the brand-level frame they work inside.

## Voice is fixed, tone moves

**Voice** is the brand's character: the same on a billboard, a receipt and a refund email. **Tone** is how loud or soft that character speaks in a given moment. A dentist's voice can be warm and plain everywhere; its tone is lighter on a cleaning reminder and steady and careful on a bill dispute.

Write down the voice once. Write tone as a table of moments (below), because that is where writers actually get stuck.

## Place the brand on four sliders

Mark each from 1 to 5. These are positions, not good-or-bad scores; the point is to make the choice visible and to catch two writers pulling in opposite directions.

| Slider | 1 | 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | talks like a colleague | talks like an institution |
| Weight | light, quick, may joke | serious, measured, never jokes |
| Words | everyday words, short sentences | technical terms, precise and dense |
| Energy | steady, understated | eager, emphatic |

Most brands cluster in the middle; a guide that marks everything 3 has made no decision. Push at least two sliders to a 1-2 or a 4-5.

## Three traits, each with its fence

Pick three traits. A trait alone ("friendly") licenses anything, so each gets a fence: the nearby failure it must not slide into.

| Trait | Means here | Fence: stop before |
|---|---|---|
| direct | lead with the answer, then the reason | curt, or dismissive of the question |
| warm | address the reader as a person with a problem | gushing, exclamation marks, pet names |
| exact | numbers, dates, names of things | jargon the buyer does not use |

For each trait add one sentence that shows it and one that breaks it, taken from the brand's real copy where possible. The pair teaches faster than the definition.

## Tone by moment

List the moments the brand actually writes for. For each, say what the reader is feeling and how the tone shifts.

| Moment | Reader feels | Tone shift |
|---|---|---|
| first visit, home page | curious, comparing | most confident; one clear claim |
| pricing | wary of a catch | plainest; no adjectives on prices |
| form error | slowed down | short, says how to fix it, no blame |
| outage or delay | annoyed or worried | sober; what happened, what next, when |
| welcome email | hopeful | warmest; one next step |
| legal, terms | careful | plain words over legal ones where the law allows |

## Words we use, words we avoid

Keep a short list both ways: the brand's own names for its products and customers (one spelling, one capitalisation), and the words it avoids, each with the replacement. Add the house stock-word ban from `../../website/design-rules.md` § 2. Anti-generic lint. A list of 10-20 lines is used; a list of 200 is ignored.

## Messaging: what we say, in what order

Voice is how; messaging is what. Write these from the owner's facts, never invented:

- **Purpose** (one sentence): the change the business exists to make for its customers. Not a slogan.
- **Promise** (one sentence): what a buyer gets that the nearest alternative does not give them. If a competitor could say the same sentence, it is not a promise yet.
- **Positioning** (one paragraph): for whom, in what situation, compared with what, and the reason to believe it. Fill it in as plain prose; do not leave the scaffold showing on a page.
- **Main message**: the line the home page leads with, built from the promise.
- **Three to five proof points**: each a claim plus its evidence (a number, a named client, a guarantee, a process). A proof point without evidence moves to the wish list.

### Lengths

Write the pitch at three lengths and keep them consistent:

| Length | Use | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| one line (under 15 words) | page title, social bio, ad headline | the promise |
| three sentences | meta description, intro paragraph | promise, who it is for, one proof |
| one minute spoken (about 150 words) | about page, sales call | positioning plus two or three proofs and a next step |

### By audience

If the brand sells to more than one group (owners and staff, buyers and users, parents and students), keep the main message and change the lead proof point for each group. Write a row per audience: what they care about first, the proof that answers it, the word they use for the problem.

## Testing a message

Run each core message through these before it goes in the guide:

1. **Swap test.** Put a competitor's name in. If it still reads true, it is not ours.
2. **Proof test.** Point to the evidence for every claim. No evidence, no claim.
3. **Read-aloud test.** Say it to someone outside the company. If they ask "meaning what?", rewrite.
4. **Shortest form.** Cut words until meaning breaks, then put the last one back.
5. **Voice check.** Does it sit where the four sliders say it should?

## What goes in the guide

The voice section of `brand-guidelines-template.md` takes: the four slider positions, the three traits with fences and examples, the tone-by-moment table, the word lists, and the messaging block (purpose, promise, positioning, proof points, the three pitch lengths). Keep before-and-after rewrites of two or three real lines; they are the most-read part.
