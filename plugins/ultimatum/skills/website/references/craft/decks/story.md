# Deck story: point, arc and slide list

Read first, before choosing a layout. Most weak decks are weak because nobody wrote down what the audience should believe, decide or do when it ends.

## 1. Write three lines before anything else

- **Audience:** who is in the room, what they already know, and what they are worried about.
- **The point:** one sentence the audience should be able to repeat afterwards. "We are raising $2M to take our clinic scheduling tool from 40 to 400 clinics in 18 months" is a point; "Company overview" is not.
- **The ask:** what you want them to do next (invest, sign, approve the budget, try it this week). If there is no ask, it is a report; send a document instead.

Show these three lines to the owner and agree them before drafting slides.

## 2. Pick the deck type

| Type | Usual length | What carries it | The ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investor pitch (seed) | 10–14 slides | the problem being painful, proof people want it, why this team | a meeting or a cheque |
| Investor pitch (later round) | 12–20 slides | numbers: growth, retention, unit economics | the round on stated terms |
| Sales deck | 8–12 slides | the buyer's problem in their words, proof from similar customers | a trial, a pilot, a signature |
| Product launch | 6–10 slides | one demo moment and what it changes | try it, write about it |
| Product demo | 4–8 slides around a live demo | the demo; slides only frame it | next step in the sale |
| Board or team update | 6–12 slides | what changed since last time, against the plan | decisions on named items |
| Conference talk | 1 slide per minute or fewer | one idea, told with examples | remember one thing |
| Internal proposal | 5–8 slides | the cost of doing nothing, the option chosen, the risk | approval |

A deck that will be read without a speaker (emailed, sent ahead) needs more words per slide than one that is presented. Decide which it is; if both, make two versions, or put the reading text in the speaker notes and export notes with the PDF.

## 3. Arcs that work

**Problem to plan** (pitches, proposals): the situation today → what is broken and who pays for it → what we do → proof it works → why now and why us → the plan and the numbers → the ask.

**Contrast** (launches, keynotes): move between how things are and how they could be, several times, so each "could be" lands harder; end on the new normal, not on a feature list. This back-and-forth is how many strong talks hold attention.

**Before, after, bridge** (sales): the buyer's day today → their day with the problem gone → the product as the way from one to the other → proof from a customer like them.

**Status against plan** (updates): headline result first → what went to plan → what did not, and why → what we need decided → next period.

Put the most important slide where attention is highest: the opening two and the closing one. Do not spend slide two on an agenda.

## 4. A seed pitch, slide by slide

1. Title: name, one-line description a stranger understands, presenter, date.
2. Problem: who has it, how often, what it costs them. A number or a quote, not adjectives.
3. Solution: what the product does, shown (screenshot or one-line demo), not described.
4. Proof: users, revenue, growth, retention, pilots, a letter of intent; whatever is strongest and true.
5. Market: who buys, how many of them, what they pay. Count from the bottom up (customers × price), and say how you counted.
6. Business model: who pays, how much, how often.
7. Competition: the honest alternatives, including "do nothing" and spreadsheets, and the one axis where you win.
8. Go to market: how the next 100 customers find you.
9. Team: why these people for this problem. Relevant facts only.
10. The ask: amount, what it pays for, the milestones it reaches.

Optional, after the ask or in an appendix: financial plan, product roadmap, deeper metrics.

## 5. Headlines are sentences

Each slide's headline states the slide's conclusion, so that reading only the headlines tells the whole story.

| Topic label (weak) | Conclusion (strong) |
|---|---|
| Market | 31,000 independent clinics in the US still book by phone |
| Results | Pilot clinics cut no-shows from 18% to 7% in three months |
| Team | We built scheduling at a hospital chain for six years |
| Pricing | Clinics pay $149 a month and earn it back in two saved visits |

Test: read the headlines alone, in order. If the story does not hold, fix the headlines before the slides.

## 6. Copy patterns, used plainly

These help draft a headline or a sequence. Use the shape, not the hype.

- **Problem, cost, fix:** name the problem, show what it costs, then the fix. Good for slide 2 to 3.
- **Before, after, bridge:** see the arc above. Good for a sales story or a case study slide.
- **Feature, so what:** every feature is followed by what it lets the user do and what that is worth. If you cannot write the "so what", cut the feature.
- **Cost of doing nothing:** what happens in six months if they do not act. Good just before the ask.
- **Number with a comparison:** "7%, down from 18%" beats "7%". A number without a baseline means nothing.
- **Quote with a name:** a real customer's words, with name and role, and permission to use them.

Leave out: invented numbers, "revolutionary", "seamless", "world-class", and any claim you could not defend if asked "how do you know?".

## 7. The slide list

Before design, write the deck as a plain list: number, headline sentence, the one thing on the slide (chart, screenshot, quote, three numbers), and the speaker note in one line. Agree it with the owner. Changing a list costs a minute; changing designed slides costs an hour.
